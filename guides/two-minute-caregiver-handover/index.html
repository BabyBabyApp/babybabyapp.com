BABYBABY GUIDES / SHARED CARE

A two-minute handover between caregivers.

When someone else takes over, give them the next useful facts—not a perfect account of the whole day.

By BabyBaby · October 22, 2026 · 5 min read

In this guide

One caregiver is leaving; another is holding the baby and looking for the bottle. The useful handover is usually smaller than the story of the entire day. It answers four questions: What just happened? What is likely to come next? Is there anything the next person must watch or do differently? How can they reach you?

This is a script for a familiar caregiver who already knows the baby and the home. A new sitter needs more than two minutes. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends meeting the sitter beforehand, showing them the home, and discussing feeding, sleep, allergies, and emergency contacts. Do that fuller introduction before relying on a quick update.

Set up the standing plan once.

Keep a short care sheet somewhere the caregiver can find it. Include the baby's name, your address, parent or guardian phone numbers, a backup contact, your child's clinician, and the local emergency number. Note any known allergies, specific needs, and the agreed plan for medication or care. The AAP's sitter checklist includes these contacts and asks parents to discuss allergies and routines before leaving.

Show a new caregiver where supplies and the sleep space are. If they will feed your baby, demonstrate your actual preparation and feeding routine; the AAP advises giving caregivers specific feeding instructions and, for an unfamiliar caregiver, having them observe a meal first. If your child has a medical plan or needs medicine, leave the clinician's or pharmacist's instructions in writing. A quick spoken summary is not enough for a dose or an emergency response plan.

Then use the same four-part handover each time.

  1. Now: “The last feed was at __; the last sleep ended at __; the last diaper change was at __.” Include only details the next caregiver will use, such as what was offered or whether there is a prepared bottle. If you did not record something, say so rather than guessing.
  2. Next: “The next likely need is __. The supplies are __.” Treat this as an expectation, not a rigid schedule. A baby may need something different from what happened yesterday.
  3. Watch: “Today I noticed __. Please call me if __.” Mention a current concern, an allergy or an agreed care instruction that could change what the caregiver does. If there is no special issue today, “nothing new” is useful too.
  4. Reach me: “I'll be at __ and expect to return at __. Call __ if you cannot reach me.” Make sure the next caregiver can actually find the standing emergency sheet.
Copyable version:
Now: last feed __; last sleep __; last diaper __.
Next: likely need __; supplies __.
Watch: today's change or concern __; call me if __.
Contact: my number __; backup __; return time __.

Use a paper note, a message, or a shared log. Say the safety-critical part aloud and invite one question before you leave. A handover works only when the receiving person knows what the words mean and can act on them.

Do not let routine hide safety details.

For a baby who still needs an infant sleep space, point out where that space is and confirm the plan for every nap. The CDC advises placing babies on their backs for all sleep times, on a firm, flat surface, with soft bedding and toys out of the sleep area. The next caregiver should know the same plan, even if they have cared for other babies before.

Be equally clear about what the caregiver may offer to eat or drink, known allergies, and any medicine. The AAP tells sitters not to give food or medicine without the parents' instructions. For a child with an individual care plan, use that plan and confirm the caregiver understands it; do not improvise instructions from a general article.

When something changes, stop and reset.

The four-part script is for ordinary handovers. If a baby is unwell, a new symptom appears, a medication is due, or the caregiver is unfamiliar with a specific need, slow down. Share the written plan, confirm who will call the clinician if needed, and make sure the receiving caregiver is comfortable taking over. If you cannot give a safe handover, postpone leaving or arrange someone who can follow the care plan.

At the other end of the shift, ask the caregiver for the same essentials back: what was offered, what sleep happened, any diaper or care detail worth knowing, and anything that needs follow-up. It is also fine to say, “I was busy and didn't log that.” An honest gap is better than a precise-sounding guess.

Keep one place for the running details.

A note on the fridge may be enough. If your family prefers an app, BabyBaby's Family Sharing can give one linked partner or caregiver a shared view of daily logs when the owner has Premium or the separate Family Sharing plan. It can save the next person from hunting through messages for a feeding or sleep entry. Still make the short verbal handover: a timeline cannot tell someone which concern matters most today or whether they understood the safety plan.

The goal is not a perfect record. It is a calmer handoff in which the next person knows where to start, what could change the plan, and how to get help.
