Tummy time when your baby hates it: a gentler start.

If two minutes on the mat ends in tears, you have not failed tummy time. Here are small ways to try again.

By BabyBaby · September 23, 2026 · 6 min read

You lay out the mat, get down to your baby's eye level, and before you've finished saying hello, they're telling you they have had enough. For some babies, lying on their tummy feels unfamiliar. A short attempt still counts as a chance to play together. You can pause and try a different position later.

Tummy time simply means supervised play while your baby is awake and on their tummy. It can happen on the floor, across your lap, or on your chest while you are fully awake. It is separate from sleep: always place your baby on their back to sleep. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) makes that distinction clear.

Why bother with tummy time?

On their tummy, babies get a different view and a chance to use the muscles in their neck, back, and shoulders. This kind of awake play supports the movement skills they gradually develop; it also changes where pressure falls on the back of the head. The NHS explains the strength and play benefits, and the AAP explains the connection with head shape.

It is not an exercise test. Your baby does not have to lift their head high or last a certain number of minutes for you to enjoy a moment together.

Begin with a few minutes, then build gently.

The AAP suggests starting soon after your baby comes home from the hospital with two or three short sessions of three to five minutes while your baby is awake and watched. As they grow and become more comfortable, its guidance is to work gradually toward 15 to 30 minutes in total each day by seven weeks. Those are totals spread across the day, not one long session. Read the AAP's full guidance.

Try one easy cue: after a diaper change or a nap, get down face to face for a short try. If your baby has had enough, stop. Another small chance later is fine.

The point of these numbers is to give you somewhere to begin, not to turn play into a stopwatch. Your baby's comfort and any advice from their own care professional matter more than completing a chart.

Three ways to make it feel more like play.

  1. Start close, on your chest. Recline while you are wide awake, place your baby tummy down on your chest, and let them look toward your face. Talk or sing to them. This can be a gentler first position than the floor. The NHS recommends chest time from birth, with the important condition that you are awake and unlikely to fall asleep.
  2. Try your lap. With your baby's tummy across your lap, support their head and keep it aligned with their body. The AAP describes lap time as another way to explore this position.
  3. Come down to their level on the floor. Put your face or a toy where they can see it. Say what you see; wait for a glance or a little movement. The AAP suggests placing yourself or a toy in reach when a baby is unsure about the position. See its play suggestions.

You do not need special equipment. Your attention is the interesting part.

What if they cry almost immediately?

Pick them up and offer comfort. Try another short session when they are awake and settled, or use your chest or lap instead of the mat. The NHS notes that many babies do not enjoy floor tummy time at first and suggests trying shorter, more interactive versions.

If tummy time is consistently difficult, or your baby was born prematurely, has reflux disease, or has another health need, ask your pediatrician or other qualified care professional for advice suited to your baby. The AAP specifically recommends individual advice in these situations.

Notice the little changes, without turning them into a score.

Maybe your baby looks toward your voice today. In a few weeks, perhaps they hold their head up for a moment or spend a little longer looking around. These are things you can notice and enjoy, even if the session was brief.

The CDC lists holding the head up while on the tummy as a milestone most babies do by two months. “Most” means at least 75% in the CDC's milestone framework; it is not a pass-or-fail test for an individual baby. If your baby loses a skill, is not meeting a milestone, or something worries you, the CDC says to speak with your child's doctor rather than wait.

A first wobbly head lift can be a lovely thing to remember. If you like saving these small moments, BabyBaby's memories and milestones give you a place for a photo and a few words. The play itself matters much more than recording it.

Keep the boundary simple: awake, watched, and back to sleep.

Stay with your baby throughout tummy time. If they become sleepy, put them down on their back in their safe sleep space. Never leave a baby to sleep on their tummy as a way to extend tummy time. The AAP's safe sleep and tummy time guidance covers both parts of that routine.

This guide offers general play ideas, not an individual developmental assessment. Follow your child's care professional if they have given you different advice.
