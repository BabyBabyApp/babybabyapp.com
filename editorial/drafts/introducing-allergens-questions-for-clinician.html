Introducing allergens: questions for your child's clinician.

A short question list can turn a confusing collection of feeding advice into a conversation about your own baby. Start with their history, then ask for practical next steps.

By BabyBaby · December 3, 2026 · 5 min read

In this guide

Advice about egg, peanut, and other foods can sound like a set of deadlines. Before turning it into a calendar, ask which guidance applies to your baby. This guide helps you prepare that conversation; it does not set an introduction schedule, diagnose an allergy, or replace an existing care plan.

A possible severe reaction needs help now. Sudden swelling of the mouth, lips, tongue, or throat; trouble breathing; blue, grey, or unusually pale skin; or a baby who becomes limp or does not respond normally are reasons to call your local emergency number immediately. If prescribed emergency allergy medicine is available, use it as directed and call for an ambulance even if your baby improves. See the NHS emergency advice for food allergy. Do not wait to complete a note or take a photograph.

Start with the history, not a shopping list.

Tell the clinician your baby's age, what foods they have already tried, any diagnosed allergies, and any eczema or previous suspected reactions. Mention allergies in the household, too, so you can discuss how to keep everyone safe.

The NHS advises talking to a GP or health visitor first when a baby has eczema, a diagnosed allergy, or a family allergy history. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) describes different peanut-introduction advice for babies with severe eczema or egg allergy. A general feeding article cannot decide which advice your child needs.

If this applies to your baby, ask for advice promptly rather than choosing an early start or a long delay yourself. An existing allergy plan takes priority over a new idea from a website.

Five questions worth taking with you.

1. What should we do next, and when?

Ask: “Given my baby's age, feeding skills, eczema, and allergy history, when should we introduce these foods? Is there anything we should discuss before starting?”

For peanut specifically, the AAP explains that assessment and the setting for a first introduction can depend on a baby's situation. Ask whether any evaluation or supervised introduction is needed, and what to do while waiting for advice. Do not assume that every baby needs testing or that another family's plan fits yours.

2. Can you show us a suitable preparation?

Ask for an example using foods your family actually eats: “What texture and preparation suit my baby's current skills? What amount should we offer, and how should we introduce it?” Write down the answer in practical terms you could explain to another caregiver.

Allergy prevention and choking prevention need separate attention. The CDC lists whole or chopped nuts and seeds, and chunks or spoonfuls of nut or seed butter, as choking hazards. Ask the clinician to demonstrate an appropriate alternative. The CDC also advises that children sit upright and are watched throughout eating.

3. What should we watch for, and who should we call?

Ask: “Which symptoms mean emergency help? Which mean medical advice? How long should we observe, and what should we do if symptoms appear later?” Ask for local contact details and a written action plan if one is needed.

The NHS notes that food-allergy symptoms can appear straight after eating or days later. A symptom diary cannot confirm the cause, and a quiet first few minutes does not answer every allergy question. Tell the clinician what you saw rather than naming the diagnosis yourself.

4. If a food is tolerated, what happens after the first taste?

The NHS recommends keeping introduced, tolerated allergenic foods in a baby's usual diet. Ask: “What does that mean for this food in our family? What preparation and frequency should we use, and what if it is missed or refused?” A plan should be clear enough to follow without forcing your baby to eat.

5. How do we make the plan work for everyone caring for our baby?

Ask how to share the advice with a partner, grandparent, nursery, or other caregiver. If someone at home has a food allergy, ask about safe storage, preparation, and cleaning as well as your baby's meal.

Before the conversation ends, agree who will offer a new food, where the instructions will be kept, and whom to contact if a question comes up. Paper or a shared note is enough.

Bring a short, factual note.

You do not need a complete feeding history. A small set of details gives you a place to start:

  • Your baby's age, diagnosed conditions, and any current allergy instructions or medicines.
  • Foods already eaten and whether you noticed any symptoms.
  • For a concern: the food and ingredients, its preparation, roughly how much was eaten, and the time.
  • What happened next, when it began, how long it lasted, and any care or treatment given.
  • Your main question: for example, “Should we have advice before trying this food?”

Keep the packaging or ingredient list if available. Say when a time or amount is approximate; filling gaps with guesses makes the story less clear. Bring existing photographs only if they are relevant and were taken without delaying care.

The NHS says a clinician may ask for a food and symptoms diary. Use it to support the conversation, not to test a suspected allergy at home.

If you think a food caused a reaction.

The British Society for Allergy & Clinical Immunology advises stopping the food and seeking medical advice if allergy is suspected during introduction. Ask for advice before offering that food again. For the emergency signs above, call for emergency help immediately; for other concerns, contact your clinician or local medical advice service.

Do not remove a whole group of other foods on your own. The NHS warns that cutting out a major food such as milk without advice can leave a child short of nutrients. Ask what to avoid, what can continue, and whether a dietitian should help.

Leave with instructions you can repeat.

Try reading the plan back: “Our next step is ____. We will use this preparation: ____. If we notice ____, we will ____. If we are unsure, we will contact ____.” Ask the clinician to correct anything you have misunderstood.

You do not have to solve every feeding question in one appointment. Leave knowing the next step, the reason for it, and how to get help.
