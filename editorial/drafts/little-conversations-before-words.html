The little conversations before words.

A look, a coo, a kick, and your answer can be a conversation. You do not need a script—or a baby who can talk yet.

By BabyBaby · October 29, 2026 · 5 min read

Imagine your baby watching the light move across the wall. They make a small sound; you say, “You see the light?” They look back, and you wait. It may last only a few seconds, but both of you took a turn.

Researchers at the Harvard Center on the Developing Child call these responsive, back-and-forth exchanges “serve and return”. The baby might begin with a sound, gesture, expression, or movement; an adult responds. These interactions are part of the relationship around a child, and support early communication and social development. They are not a lesson you have to schedule.

A conversation does not need words on both sides.

Babies communicate long before they use a first word. A sound can be a turn, but so can a reaching hand, a smile, or sustained interest in a face or object. The CDC suggests answering a baby's sounds by repeating them and adding words. You can also name what has caught their attention, sing a line, or simply smile back.

It is easy to miss a baby's reply if you fill every quiet moment. Harvard's serve-and-return guide emphasizes taking turns and waiting. After you answer, leave a little space. Your baby may look, move, make another sound, or be done for now.

Try “notice, answer, pause.”

  1. Notice what they noticed. Follow the baby's gaze, sound, or gesture rather than asking them to attend to whatever you chose. A baby staring at a spoon has already given you a topic.
  2. Answer in your own way. “That's the spoon. Clink!” is enough. You can copy a sound and add one word, or make a warm expression if talking feels awkward. The point is that your response connects to their signal.
  3. Pause for their turn. Wait without demanding a performance. If they respond, continue. If they turn away or get fussy, let the exchange end or offer comfort.
A tiny example: Baby taps the bath cup. You say, “Cup! You heard it tap.” You pause. Baby taps it again. You answer, “Tap, tap.” That is the whole game.

The rhythm is useful because it works with the child in front of you. You can add more words for an older baby, or keep it to a face and a sound with a newborn. You do not need to count turns.

Use the moments already in your day.

During a diaper change: Tell your baby what you are about to do, then answer a sound or expression if one comes. “I'm lifting your feet. There we go.” It gives a familiar routine some predictability without turning it into a running commentary.

At a window or on a walk: Name whatever your baby is watching: a moving leaf, a dog, a bus. Say one thing, pause, and see if their attention is still there. You can follow a new focus instead of finishing your explanation.

With a book: Point to the picture your baby reaches for. Make a sound or name it, then wait. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says you can follow a baby's lead and need not finish the book. Even a short look at one page counts as time together.

When you sing: Repeat a familiar line and pause. A smile, kick, or sound can be an answer. The CDC includes talking, reading, and singing among ordinary ways to interact with a baby. Your own voice matters more here than a polished song.

Keep it low-pressure for both of you.

You are not meant to narrate all day. Choose a moment when your baby is alert and you have a little attention to spare. The CDC advises watching for tiredness or fussiness and taking a break from play. The AAP likewise describes looking away or fussing as possible signs that a baby is ready to pause book sharing. Silence and rest belong in the day too.

Use the language or languages that feel natural at home. The AAP says using more than one language does not cause a speech or language delay; families can talk with children in the languages they are most comfortable using. A grandparent's word, a household rhyme, and a familiar nickname are all part of your baby's world.

Some exchanges become favorite memories. If you want to remember a particular laugh or sound, a short note or voice story in BabyBaby can keep that detail with the moment. The interaction matters whether or not you save it.

If something worries you, ask.

This guide offers ideas for connection, not a way to assess development. Babies vary, and a single quiet afternoon does not tell you much. If your baby is not responding to voices or sounds as you expect, stops making sounds or doing things they previously did, or you have another hearing or communication concern, talk with your child's clinician. The AAP encourages parents to raise those concerns rather than trying to judge a baby's progress from one milestone.

For today, you can simply notice the next small signal your baby gives you, answer it, and leave room for another turn.
